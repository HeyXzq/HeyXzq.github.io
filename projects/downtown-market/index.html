Downtown Market

For the City of Charlottesville, I turned 1,000+ customer and vendor survey responses into product, pricing, and stall-placement changes that contributed to about 15% higher vendor revenue.

Role
Marketing Specialist Intern
Organization
City of Charlottesville
Timeline
Summer 2025 · about two months
Methods
Survey design · MaxDiff · Conjoint · TURF
1,000+survey responses from customers and vendors
~15%higher vendor revenue vs. prior-quarter averages
~10%more traffic vs. prior-quarter averages
2 monthsfrom research to changes on the ground

01

The situation

Downtown Market is a city-supported public market. A newly opened market nearby was competing for the same customers and vendors.

The team had been relying on on-site observation and vendor feedback. It sensed that its crowd skewed older than the competitor's while younger shoppers still mattered, but it couldn't explain why people came, which products drove purchases, or how price, assortment, and experience affected whether they'd come back.

I defined the goal: an evidence-based product-mix and market-experience strategy that grows traffic and vendor revenue without damaging vendor relationships.

02

The approach

In the first month, I designed the customer and vendor surveys, collected 1,000+ responses, and ran the analysis. In the second month, the team put the recommendations into practice.

  1. SurveyCustomers and vendors, 1,000+ responses.
  2. MaxDiffWhich product categories matter most?
  3. ConjointHow do shoppers trade off product mix, price, experience, and vendor variety?
  4. TURFWhich small set of categories reaches the most shoppers?
  5. Vendor checkCan vendors actually make these changes?
  6. RoadmapProduct, pricing, and stall placement.

To keep the survey short enough to finish, I limited demographic questions, capped the number of choice tasks, and randomized task and option order.

03

Why three methods

MaxDiff

Simple ratings make everything look important. MaxDiff makes people pick the most and least likely purchase from small sets, which produces a real ranking.

Conjoint

Shoppers choose between whole market profiles, revealing which attributes actually drive the decision to visit and which are nice extras.

TURF

Finds the smallest set of categories that appeals to the largest share of shoppers, and how much reach each added category brings.

The methods can disagree: a category can score high on its own but add little reach to a mix that already covers those shoppers. So I used them in order: MaxDiff to rank, conjoint for trade-offs, TURF to optimize the mix, then vendor feedback to check feasibility.

04

What we found

  • Strong demand for food, beverages, snacks, prepared meals, and flowers.
  • Older shoppers showed more interest in groceries.
  • Younger shoppers were an important segment, but more price-sensitive.
  • Furniture and some handmade crafts drew weaker overall demand.

Put together, parts of the market's product mix, pricing, and stall visibility didn't match what most shoppers wanted.

05

The decision

Low demand didn't mean we should remove vendors.

Vendors pay stall fees and bring variety, occupancy, and community trust. Removing them would leave empty stalls and hurt long-term participation. So I turned the research into two low-cost changes:

Adjust the offer

Vendors adjusted selected products, portions, and prices, including lower-priced options for younger shoppers, while keeping what made them distinctive.

Rethink the layout

Food, beverage, and snack stalls moved to higher-traffic areas, while destination categories like furniture and some crafts moved to less prominent spots.

06

Results

Compared with prior-quarter averages, the changes contributed to:

~15% higher vendor revenue and ~10% more traffic.

There was no full control group, so I treat these as directional results, not proof that the research alone caused the change.

07

Looking back

What I'd do differently next time:

  • Survey people who stopped coming or never came, not just current visitors.
  • Set clear baselines before making changes.
  • Roll out in phases, or use matched control areas, to measure impact properly.
  • Track traffic, conversion, average transaction value, and vendor revenue separately.
  • Rotate placements and run themed promotions so lower-demand vendors get fairer exposure.
